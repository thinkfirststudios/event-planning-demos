The shape of the night

The door

nobody goes first

The first people through the door are the nervous ones. I keep the room dimmer than you’d think and the music lower than the DJ wants, and I put something to do within sight of the entrance, so nobody stands alone holding a coat.

If they can see the bar from the door, they’ve already relaxed.

The turn

when it tips

There’s a moment when a room stops being people standing nicely and starts being a party. It rarely happens on its own. Food lands, a lamp comes down, the music moves on, and the whole room tips. I call it from the floor.

Nobody notices the turn. That’s how you know it went right.

The last hour

is the whole job

Everyone remembers the end of a night and almost nobody plans for it. Rooms empty early when the lights come up too soon or the coats turn into a queue. The people left at the end are the ones you actually wanted there, so I build the evening backwards from them. When it finishes is the room’s decision and its rules; what it feels like is mine.

Anyone can start a party. The end is where I earn the fee.

The strike

then it goes back

When the last guest has gone, the crew takes it all down: every lamp, every cable, every chair back where the room had it. Then I walk it with the room’s manager before I leave.

You leave with your shoes in your hand. I leave last.

The roomsby their problems

I don’t show you whose party it was. I tell you what kind of room it is, what’s wrong with it, and what I do about that.

A basement, low ceiling

lit from the table up

A low ceiling is a gift once you stop fighting it. I turn the overheads off and keep every lamp at table height, so the ceiling disappears and people lean in.

I put the bar where the queue would form anyway.

A back room, no windows

candles and nothing else

A room with no windows can be whatever hour you like. I take down what’s on the walls, hang cloth where the room allows it and let the table do the lighting. Open flame is the room’s call, and I ask before I plan around it.

If a room has no view, give them each other.

A top floor, no lift

the city does the rest

Everyone arrives slightly out of breath and slightly pleased with themselves. I leave the window alone and keep the inside darker than the view. No lift is a conversation about access first, and I have it with the room before anything else gets planned.

You don’t decorate a view. You get out of its way.

What I do

The room

I design it and direct the light. What comes in, what goes out, where the bar stands, where people will end up whether you plan for it or not.

Most rooms need things taken away, not added.

The shape of the night

The door, the turn, the last hour, the strike. I set how the evening moves before anyone arrives, then change it on the floor when the night has other ideas.

A plan is what you depart from on purpose.

The bar

I design where it goes and how it looks, and I work out the drinks direction with the room’s own team. They hold the licence and they pour.

I care what’s in the glass. I just don’t put it there.

The people

I cast the suppliers and the crew for the night: who’s right for this room and this crowd, not who’s next on a list.

A good crew is the only thing I’d fight you for.

The floor

I run the night on the floor, start to strike, and I’m the person everyone comes to so that you’re not.

If you can’t find me, it’s going well.

What I don’t do

  • I don’t serve alcohol and I don’t hold a liquor licence. The room holds the licence, the room’s staff pour, and the studio does not serve alcohol.
  • I don’t book the room for you. You take the room, and I’ll tell you honestly what I think of it.
  • I don’t run weddings. That’s a different job and it deserves someone who loves it.
  • I don’t do daytime conferences. I’d be bad at them.
  • I don’t take a cut of the bar. What you spend with the room stays with the room.

Better you hear all of this now than on the night.

What it costs

My fee is flat, for the night. It covers the design, the crew, running the floor and the strike. It doesn’t move with your bar spend, and it isn’t a share of anything.

The room charges you separately, usually a food-and-beverage minimum, and you pay that to the room directly. I can’t quote it, because it’s theirs.

The room’s minimum is usually the bigger number. People are surprised by that, so I say it first.

The walkthroughin the room, in the evening

We meet at the hour your night will actually happen, because a room at lunchtime tells you nothing. It takes about as long as a slow drink. You’ll hear where I’d put the bar and what I’d do about the light.

If there’s no room booked yet, we walk the kinds of room that would work instead. Either way, one page arrives afterwards: what I’d do and what my fee would be. One page, not a deck.

You’ll know what I think before you’ve finished your drink.
