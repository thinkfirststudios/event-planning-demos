In the order it happens

The arrival

nobody goes first

The first people through the door are the nervous ones. So the music’s already on, there’s something wonderful to look at from the entrance, and the room’s team are ready, so nobody stands alone holding a coat.

A party starts at the door, not at the speeches.

The turn

when it tips

There’s a moment when a room stops being people standing nicely and starts being a party. It rarely happens on its own. Food lands, the music moves on, and the whole room tips. I call it from the floor.

Nobody notices the turn. That’s how you know it went right.

The last hour

is the whole job

Everyone remembers the end of a party and almost nobody plans for it. The people left at the end are the ones you actually wanted there, so I build the day backwards from them. When it finishes is the room’s decision and its rules. What it feels like is mine.

Anyone can start a party. The end is where I earn the fee.

The strike

then it goes back

When the last guest has gone, the crew takes it all down: every flower, every cable, every chair back where the room had it. Then I walk it with the room’s manager before I leave.

You leave with your shoes in your hand. I leave last.

What I do to a room

Named by what they are, never by whose party it was

A bare hall, a courtyard, a function room with a difficult carpet. I don’t need a good room. I need a room and a yes. Then I hang something from the ceiling, flood it with colour and put people where they’ll have the best time.

Most rooms are one bold decision away from being a party.

What I do

The plan

I plan the party from the first walk round the room: what it is, who’s doing what, what it costs and in what order it all has to happen. You get it on a single page.

If it isn’t on the page, it isn’t happening.

The look

Colour, flowers, fabric, what hangs from the ceiling, what comes in and what goes out. I design it so people stop in the doorway.

If you’re going to do it, do it in colour.

The people

I find and book the suppliers and the crew: who’s right for this room and this crowd, not who’s next on a list.

A good crew is the only thing I’d fight you for.

The bar

I design where it goes and how it looks, and I work out the drinks with the room’s own team. They hold the licence and they pour.

I care what’s in the glass. I just don’t put it there.

The day

I run it on the floor, start to strike, and I’m the person everyone comes to so that you’re not.

If you can’t find me, it’s going well.

Before we start

What I don’t do

  • I don’t serve alcohol and I don’t hold a liquor licence. The room holds the licence, the room’s staff pour, and the studio does not serve alcohol.
  • I don’t book the room for you. You take the room, and I’ll tell you honestly what I think of it.
  • I don’t plan wedding ceremonies. That’s a different job. The party afterwards, and the family celebrations around it, I’d love.
  • I don’t do daytime conferences. I’d be bad at them.
  • I don’t take a cut of anything you spend with the room or anyone else. What you spend with them stays with them.

Better you hear all of this now than on the day.

In plain words

What it costs

My fee is flat, for the party. It covers the plan, the design, the crew, running the day and the strike. It doesn’t move with what you spend elsewhere, and it isn’t a share of anything.

The room charges you separately, usually a food-and-beverage minimum, and you pay that to the room directly. I can’t quote it, because it’s theirs.

The room’s minimum is usually the bigger number. People are surprised by that, so I say it first.

The first step

The walkthrough

In the room, at the hour you’ll use it

We meet in the room at the time your party will actually happen, because a room at the wrong hour tells you nothing. It takes about as long as a slow coffee. You’ll hear where everything goes and what I’d do with the colour.

If there’s no room booked yet, we walk the kinds of room that would work instead. Either way, one page arrives afterwards: what I’d do and what my fee would be. One page, not a deck.

You’ll know what I think before your coffee’s gone cold.
